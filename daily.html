🥊 tier 3 · principal — You track seventeen eval metrics. Which ones block a release, and why those?
You own the agent platform for six product teams shipping weekly in a multi-tenant service provider. The dashboard carries seventeen metrics, and releases are blocked on one composite: agent score at or above 87%. Last quarter two incidents shipped green — an approval bypass on a customer-facing change, and a latency regression that tripled P95 while the average barely moved. Teams complain that the gate is simultaneously too loose and too annoying, and one team has started shipping behind a feature flag to avoid it. A compliance audit lands in 90 days. Leadership wants fewer, sharper gates and a defensible appeal path.
  1. Kill the composite: 87% blended is exactly how a 1% safety violation disappears into a rounding error.
  2. Three classes of metric: zero-tolerance blockers, thresholded blockers, and monitored-only signals.
  3. Zero-tolerance, any occurrence blocks: policy violations, unauthorized actions, approval bypasses, cross-account data access.
  4. Thresholded: task success >95%, tool selection >98%, tool argument >99%, valid transitions 100%, grounded claims >95%, recovery >90%.
  5. Monitored-only: cost per successful task, tool and model calls per task, adoption and action rate — they inform, not block.
  6. Add an explicit P95 latency gate against the SLA, since averages hid the 3x regression.
  7. Gate per stratum, not aggregate: simple, medium, hard and adversarial pass rates reported separately.
  8. Pre-launch gates catch known regressions; canary at 5% with auto-rollback catches the unknown ones. Require both.
  9. Appeal path: thresholded gates can be waived by a named owner with an expiry date; zero-tolerance gates cannot.
  10. Publish the baseline next to every target — these are starting points calibrated to risk, not industry standards.

Numbers

  • How 87% happens: task 96, tool 99, grounding 95, latency 80, safety 99 averaged looks fine while safety's 1% is a live unauthorized action.
  • The production-ready conjunction to recite: safety violations = 0 AND policy bypasses = 0 AND task success >95% AND groundedness >95%.
  • Canary rule: 5% of traffic for 48 hours, auto-rollback if CSAT falls more than 0.3 below baseline, no overrides.
  • Model swap: 90/10 A/B for two weeks, roll to 50% after 7 flat-or-better days, roll back if any metric degrades more than 5%.

Traps

  • Gating on all seventeen — six teams will route around a gate that fails weekly on cosmetics, as one already has.
  • Keeping the composite because leadership wants one number; give them an engineering view and a business view instead.
  • Quoting targets as industry standards rather than baselines calibrated to business criticality and risk.
  • Gating only pre-launch — the happy-path eval passed at 95% and still dropped CSAT to 3.2 from 4.5.

Follow-ups

  • A zero-tolerance gate fires on a false positive and blocks all six teams. Now what?
    Fix the evaluator, never the gate. Deterministic checks are debuggable in minutes: replay the trace, confirm the transition or approval assertion, add the case to the golden set. Waiving a zero-tolerance gate once teaches teams it is negotiable.
  • How do these gates survive the compliance audit?
    The trace is the artifact: trace_id, case_id, agent, prompt and model version, per-step tool name, arguments, response, latency, error, plus per-dimension eval scores including the policy score. Approval-bypass rate of zero is only credible with a trace that shows the approval state.
  • If a team has no gates at all today, which one do you install first?
    Approval bypass = 0. It is deterministic, cheap to compute from the state machine, needs no judge, and prevents the worst outcome — an irreversible customer-facing action taken without a human. Task success and grounding can wait a sprint.
