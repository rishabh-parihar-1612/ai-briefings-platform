🥊 tier 4 · principal — Eleven teams want an agent next quarter. You can staff three. Sequence it and say no.
You lead a 4-person AI platform team at a 200-engineer company with two agents in production. Quarterly planning produced eleven agent requests: NOC triage v2, sales-email drafting, invoice reconciliation, an HR assistant, release notes, a customer-facing chat widget, log summarization, contract review, capacity forecasting, onboarding checklists, and a 'general company assistant'. Your CTO's instinct is to build an agent framework first so all eleven can self-serve. You have capacity for roughly three plus platform work. The CFO froze headcount until Q2.
  1. First I push back on the framework: we have two agents. The right abstraction is revealed by the third.
  2. Sequencing rule: impact times one-over-complexity, and each survivor needs a named owner for eighteen months.
  3. Second filter is autonomy — pick the leftmost point on the spectrum that solves it. Most of these are workflows.
  4. Third filter is blast radius. Customer-facing chat and contract review carry the real downside here.
  5. My three: NOC triage v2 (our data moat), log summarization (cheap, high volume), invoice reconciliation (clear unit of value).
  6. Published non-goals, not deferrals: sales-email drafting, autonomous contract redlines, general company assistant.
  7. Release notes is a buy, not a build — non-differentiated workflow, off-the-shelf tools already do it with HITL.
  8. Platform work in parallel is the five seams, one sprint — not a framework. Those survive all eleven requests.
  9. And the honest close: if we cannot name an owner for eighteen months, I argue we should not start it.

Numbers

  • Capacity: 4 engineers x 13 weeks = 52 engineer-weeks; the five abstraction seams cost about one sprint, leaving roughly 46 (issue 004, §18).
  • Rule of Three cost of getting it wrong: a composite team burned 6 months on a framework, then 2 more months working around it (issue 011, §17).
  • Staffing ratio check: 1 platform engineer per 8-12 product engineers means 200 engineers implies 17-25 at maturity; we have 4 (issue 005, §20).
  • Scaling inflection: we sit at the 1-to-3-team transition. The platform team formalizes at 3-to-10, not at eleven requests (issue 004, §20).

Traps

  • Saying yes to the framework. One use case plus hypothetical extension points equals wrong abstractions for agent two.
  • Ranking by requester seniority. That is how an ownerless general assistant beats the agent with a data moat.
  • Deferring the no's to next quarter. Deferral without a non-goal list means re-litigating all eleven in 90 days.
  • Taking all eleven as pilots. At four engineers you get eleven unowned prototypes and no production agent.

Follow-ups

  • The CTO insists on the platform first. How do you win that argument?
    Use the Rule of Three as a principled defence, not resistance: we are building the platform, just not prematurely — first instance specific, second copy-modify, abstract at the third. Then concede the part that is real: the five day-one seams, one sprint, because their retrofit cost is 10x.
  • Sales escalates the email-drafting no to your VP. What is your line?
    Brand risk on outbound is a business risk decision, not an engineering default — I give the trade-off honestly and let whoever owns brand decide. Separately it fails my filters: no data moat, off-the-shelf tools exist, and no named owner. If the business accepts the risk and names an owner, I re-rank it.
  • How do the eight teams you refused not become shadow AI?
    By giving them the platform instead of a queue. Self-service for standard patterns, review only for novel ones, and office hours. Shadow AI appears when the central team is a gatekeeper — the fix is making the sanctioned path the cheapest path, not policing the alternative.
